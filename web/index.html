What you are looking at

A 3D model of the Mississippi Sound — Lake Borgne to Dauphin Island. The mainland waterline is the OpenStreetMap coastline, so the bays, bayous and river mouths are the real ones, and the islands follow their OpenStreetMap shorelines (Cat, West/East Ship, Horn, Petit Bois, Dauphin, Deer, Round). Open-shelf depths come from the GEBCO 2024 grid; inside the Sound, the bays and the lagoons they are still a procedural stand-in. Neither is NOAA survey data. North is the mainland from Pearlington through Gulfport and Pascagoula to Bayou La Batre. Magenta ticks are the Louisiana and Alabama lines.

Moving around

Drag to pan east–west and north–south. Scroll to zoom. Right-drag (or two fingers) to tilt. Arrow keys pan once the map has focus.

Not a photograph

The surface is a heightfield. Each PNG tile stores depth in its RGB values (Mapbox terrain-RGB encoding). The GPU lifts every vertex from that depth and tints it by depth (hypsometric colour, hillshade, optional contours).

Zoom levels 6–14 are the same idea as a web map: more tiles, finer ground sample. They are not extra pictures of the same view.

Where the depths come from

South of the barrier chain the floor is the GEBCO 2024 global grid, clipped to this chart and bundled with the build so the viewer runs with the network unplugged. GEBCO's 15 arc-second cells are about 460 m across, which is too coarse to resolve the Sound, the bays and the lagoons — those are still a procedural stand-in, and the two are blended across the shelf edge.

The surface is therefore modified GEBCO, not the published grid. It is not for navigation, and it is not NOAA survey data — it must not be presented as unaltered NOAA bathymetry or as National Bathymetric Source.

Ocean overlay

A HYCOM surface-current forecast stack can be drawn as particles. The particle motion is visually exaggerated: it shows the shape of the field, not a parcel of water moving at speed. The field is drawn at the current forecast hour and repaints as the clock moves. This viewer is not an official NOAA product. Surface currents courtesy of the HYCOM consortium (dataset id when present). Buoy observations are NOAA NDBC open data.

Reading a station

Each NDBC station is drawn as a glyph at its position, with a wind barb when it reported wind. The glyph is the platform, taken from NDBC's own station table rather than guessed: a hollow circle is a moored buoy and a hollow diamond a DART — both float — while a filled square is a fixed shore or pier station and a filled triangle an oil platform. In this box that separation is most of the story: only a handful of marks are genuinely offshore instruments, and the rest are tide gauges and met stations along the coast.

Marks fade with the age of their last observation — full strength under an hour, dimmer to six hours, faintest beyond. NDBC leaves dead stations in its table indefinitely, so this box routinely carries instruments that last reported weeks ago; without the fade they would be drawn exactly like a reading from twenty minutes back. Hover or focus a mark and the inspector names the platform and states the age outright.

Aircraft overlay

Live public ADS-B positions over this box appear as heading marks, each drawn at its reported barometric altitude with a leader down to its position on the water. Aircraft are at true altitude — the vertical exaggeration slider stretches the seafloor only, never the sky, so a tilted view shows the real gap between a helicopter on the platform run and an airliner overhead.
